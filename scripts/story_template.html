Ten years of pedestrian collisions in Athens

Where pedestrians were hit in Athens, which streets kept coming back year after year, what's around them, and how far help was.

By Eriola Trungu Impersimi · from my MSc thesis in Geoinformatics (NTUA, 2021), re-analysed in Python in 2026

1 · The numbers

A decade that got safer, unevenly

Between 2008 and 2017 the police recorded 1,318 collisions in the Municipality of Athens where a pedestrian was hurt. Most of them, 1,208, were slight injuries. Eighty pedestrians were seriously injured and thirty were killed. In 68 collisions two people on foot were hurt, and in nine of them three or four.

Until 2011 there were about 180 a year. Then the number dropped quickly, to 98 in 2013 and 81 in 2017. The economic crisis took a lot of cars off the road: car numbers were reported to have fallen by more than 58% in 2011. Greece also invested in road safety in those years, and in 2021 it received the European Transport Safety Council's award for halving road deaths between 2010 and 2020.

The most serious collisions fell even faster, from 15 killed or seriously injured in 2008 to two in 2017. With so few, single years jump around a lot, so in the maps below I use all ten years together.

The streets where it keeps happening

A pedestrian can only be hit on a street, so I measured the pattern along the streets. As you scroll, the map changes.

Each dot is one collision, coloured by how badly the pedestrian was hurt. You can see straight away that they follow the big avenues and the busy centre: Patision, Athinas, Peiraios, Alexandras and Syggrou.

Density along the streets

In the thesis I used kernel density, which spreads each collision out in a circle, over buildings and squares too. This time I measured the density along the streets. I cut every street into 50 m pieces, and each collision counts for the pieces within 250 m walking distance. The brightest pieces have about seven collisions per kilometre of street a year.

The centre around Omonia

The densest street in Athens is Patision, near Omonia and Aiolou, with 25 collisions within 150 m in ten years. Athinas, Peiraios and the start of 28is Oktovriou come right after. These streets are busy at all hours and full of shops, and people cross wherever they can, not only at the crossings.

Where people were killed or seriously injured

The 109 most serious collisions show a different map. They follow the wide, fast avenues: Alexandras, Vasilissis Sofias and Galatsiou, the same as I found in the thesis. The faster the car, the worse the injury.

    3 · Over time

    Persistent, new and fading hotspots

    A place that is a hotspot every year needs a different response from a place that only became one recently. In the thesis I used ArcGIS's space-time cube for this. This time I rebuilt the same method in Python, on the same 200 m hexagons.

    Hotspots, year by year

    For every year, I tested each hexagon to see if it's a significant hotspot (Getis-Ord Gi*), and then checked its trend over the ten years with a Mann-Kendall test. 20 hexagons are persistent hotspots, which means they were hot in at least nine of the ten years. Thirteen of them are around Omonia, the same as in the thesis.

    PersistentConsecutiveNewSporadic

    The north is where it grew

    More than half of the new and consecutive hotspots, the ones that only became hot in the last years, are in the northern districts: along Patision towards Galatsiou, and around Kypseli. In the thesis these areas came out as intensifying too. This is where action now would help the most.

    Clusters and outliers

    Local Moran's I checks whether a hexagon is similar to its neighbours. 90 high-high hexagons form the main clusters, with a lot of collisions surrounded by a lot of collisions. The few high-low hexagons are single problem spots in otherwise quiet streets.

    High-highHigh-lowLow-highLow-low

    The centre hardly moves

    The centre of each year's collisions hardly moves. It stays within a few hundred metres, just north-east of Omonia towards Exarcheia, and moves a little north in the later years, because collisions fell faster in the centre than in the northern neighbourhoods.

    4 · On the ground

    Ten places I looked at

    For the thesis I looked at ten of the worst places on Street View: eight persistent and two intensifying hotspots. They have a lot in common: several lanes, traffic lights and crossings, shops, bus stops, and people crossing wherever they can. Each card links to the Street View image I used in the thesis. The number is how many collisions there were within 150 m in this re-analysis.

    5 · What's around

    Busy places, not bars

    In the thesis I looked at four kinds of places around the collisions: alcohol venues (bars, pubs, clubs), education (schools and colleges), traffic points (crossings, signals, stops) and transport points (bus, tram and metro stops, taxi ranks). First, I counted how many of each are within 100, 250 and 500 m walking distance of a collision.

    Then, like in the thesis, I made bivariate maps. A bivariate map shows two things at once: here, how many collisions a hexagon has, and how many of one kind of place. The two colour scales mix, so the dark purple hexagons are where both are high. They're my favourite maps from the thesis.

    Collisions and bars

    Most hexagons have no bars at all, so most of the map is grey or pink. The dark hexagons, with many bars and many collisions, are in the centre, around Psyrri, Monastiraki, Syntagma and Exarcheia, with a few towards Syggrou and Ampelokipoi. The thesis found almost the same places.

    Collisions and transport stops

    With bus, tram and metro stops, the dark hexagons spread much further: around Omonia, but also along Patision and in Exarcheia. Where there are stops, there are people crossing.

    Collisions and traffic points

    Crossings and traffic signals give the most dark hexagons of all: 102 hexagons with many of both, all over the city, but mostly in the centre, Patisia, Kypseli and Ampelokipoi.

    Collisions and schools

    Schools are the opposite. Only 6 hexagons have many schools and many collisions, and they're scattered around. In the thesis the link with education was weak too.

    Bivariate Local Moran's I

    In the thesis I also used bivariate Local Moran's I. It asks whether hexagons with many traffic points are surrounded by hexagons with many collisions. The high-high clusters are around Omonia and Exarcheia, and then in Ampelokipoi and Patisia.

    High-highHigh-lowLow-highLow-low

    And for bars

    For bars the high-high clusters are much smaller, only 16 hexagons, mostly around Omonia and Exarcheia. Bars and collisions meet in the centre, but not much anywhere else.

    But a map can't tell us if bars cause collisions. The centre has many bars and many people, buses and crossings, so bars would be close to collisions even if they had nothing to do with them. So I also tested it in a fairer way. I counted the collisions in each 200 m hexagon, took into account how much street it has, and put all four kinds of place into one model together. This is a negative binomial regression, the usual model for counts like these.

    Once I took into account how busy a place is, bars and schools no longer explained where collisions happen. Crossings, signals and bus stops did, because that's where people and traffic meet.

    That doesn't mean crossings are dangerous. They're where people cross. It means the risk follows how many people meet traffic, so the fix is in how those meeting points are designed. In the thesis I then used Geographically Weighted Regression to see where each link is strongest. The map below does the same for traffic points.

    Where traffic points matter most

    Geographically Weighted Regression gives every hexagon its own rate ratio. The darker the hexagon, the more collisions come with more crossings and signals there. Hexagons that aren't significant are left blank.

    And transport stops

    The same for bus, tram and metro stops. The link is significant across much of the city, and most often in the northern districts.

    6 · Reaching help

    How far help was

    The thesis ended with an emergency dashboard: collisions, hospitals, police and fire stations, and Esri's live traffic layer, so a control room could see how long an ambulance might take. This time the street network does that work. For every collision I measured the drive along the streets to the nearest public general hospital, police station and fire station, at an average of 30 km/h and with no traffic.

    Athens has one extra complication: the public hospitals take turns on emergency duty (εφημερίες), so the nearest hospital that will take a patient changes from day to day. In the dashboard you can tick which hospitals are on duty, and all the times on the map are recalculated.

    Minutes to the nearest hospital

    Here the streets are coloured by the drive to the nearest public general hospital, if they're all open. The eastern districts, near Evangelismos, Ippokrateio, Laiko and Elpis, are only a few minutes away. The far west, around Kolonos, Sepolia, Iera Odos and Athinon Avenue, is the furthest, with a median of about 8 minutes, against under 1 minute in the east.

    When only one hospital is on duty

    If Evangelismos were the only hospital on duty, collisions in the far west of the municipality would be over 10 minutes away, even with no traffic. That's why the rota matters, and with traffic it would be even longer.

    Fire and rescue

    OpenStreetMap shows only two fire stations in or near the municipality, so these times are the worst case. If stations are missing from the data, the real times are shorter.

    7 · What would help

    What the evidence points to

    In the thesis I ended with three suggestions, and this re-analysis supports them:

    Redesign the places where people and traffic meet. The persistent hotspots are wide streets with many lanes, where crossings are far apart and people cross in between. Narrower crossings, refuge islands, more green time for pedestrians and lower speeds on the avenues would help where the risk actually is.

    Look at the north now. While the centre got better, new and consecutive hotspots appeared around Patision, Galatsiou and Kypseli.

    Behaviour matters too. Drivers often don't give way, and many people cross wherever they like. Education and enforcement need to go together with better design.

    What the data can't tell us: it only has the year of each collision, not the day or the hour, the age of the pedestrian or the type of vehicle. With those, the same methods could separate night-time collisions near bars from school-run collisions, like my London project does with the British police data.

The distances are the best case: the thesis street network has no one-way information, so routes can use streets in both directions. Hospitals, police and fire stations come from OpenStreetMap. Public hospitals that are only building outlines in OpenStreetMap are placed at the bus stop named after them, next to their entrance.
